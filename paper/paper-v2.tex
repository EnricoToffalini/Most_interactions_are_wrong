\title{The Link Function Problem in Psychological Interaction Testing}


\shorttitle{Link Functions and Interactions}


\usepackage{etoolbox}









\authorsnames[{1},{2},{1},{1},{1}]{Margherita Calderan,Filippo

\leftheader{Calderan, Gambarota, Sità, Feraco and Toffalini}



\abstract{Equal differences on one scale become different differences on
another scale. This is crucial for interaction testing because an
interaction is a difference between differences, and in psychological
research the observed response and the construct of interest are often
expressed on different scales. Therefore, the same data can show an
interaction on one scale and not on another. Generalized Linear Models
make this problem explicit by separating two decisions, the family,
which describes the nature of the response variable, and the link
function, which sets the scale on which effects are additive. Because a
model without an interaction term is additive on the link scale,
choosing a link is choosing what `no interaction' means. The paper has
two connected parts. First, a preregistered review of recent articles
from five prominent psychology journals shows that interaction testing
is routinely performed in research, that the link function is most often
unspecified, and that constrained outcomes are frequently analyzed with
Gaussian identity-link models. Second, worked examples and simulations
examine forced-choice accuracy with a chance floor, the choice between
logit and probit links, and sum scores as a measurement case, and
quantify how a plausible-looking link can turn an additive structure on
one scale into a statistically significant interaction on another. A
final diagnostic simulation shows that residual checks, a link test, and
AIC comparisons give uneven protection and do not by themselves decide
which scale the scientific claim concerns. Interaction claims remain
underspecified until researchers state, justify, and probe the scale on
which no interaction is assumed. }

\keywords{interactions, moderation, generalized linear models, link
functions, model misspecification}

\subsection{}\label{section}

We start with a simplified example. Two groups of children, for instance
a clinical and a neurotypical group, differ in how many errors they make
on a task, and in both groups errors decrease with age. Imagine a
researcher who records error counts from children aged six to ten and
analyzes them, as is common, with a Gaussian linear model that includes
an age-by-group interaction. In Figure~\ref{fig-simple-count-example}
(Panel A) the two fitted lines decline at different rates, and the gap
between the groups is much wider at age six than at age ten. Is there an
age-by-group interaction?

\begin{figure}[h]

\caption{\label{fig-simple-count-example}Simulated error counts for two
groups of children aged 6 to 10, generated with the same age effect in
both groups on the log of the expected error count. A Gaussian linear
model fitted to the counts produces straight lines with different
slopes, which suggests an age-by-group interaction. A Poisson model with
a log link fitted to the same data produces curves that converge with
age on the count scale and parallel lines on the log scale (where points
show the log of non-zero counts), so the group gap shrinks on the count
scale and stays constant on the log scale. Whether the pattern is an
interaction depends on the scale on which effects are assumed to add.}

\centering{

\pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio]{paper-v2_files/figure-pdf/fig-simple-count-example-1.pdf}}

}

\end{figure}%

An interaction is a difference between differences, so the question is
whether the group difference itself changes with age. Since the gap
narrows as the children grow older, the natural conclusion is that age
and group interact. The data in Figure~\ref{fig-simple-count-example},
however, were generated without any interaction. Errors follow a Poisson
process in which age and group add on the log scale, so the two groups
share the same age slope on that scale. A Poisson model with a log link
recovers this structure, and on the log scale its fitted lines are
parallel (Figure~\ref{fig-simple-count-example}, Panel B). Mapped back
onto the count scale, the same model produces two curves that converge
with age, because a constant difference on the log scale is a constant
ratio on the count scale, and the absolute gap between the groups
therefore narrows as errors decline
(Figure~\ref{fig-simple-count-example}, Panel C). Whether an interaction
is present thus depends on the scale on which effects are assumed to
add. Additivity on the log scale implies non-parallel curves on the
count scale, and additivity on the count scale would imply non-parallel
curves on the log scale.

This ambiguity extends far beyond this single illustration, as
interaction hypotheses are widespread in psychological research and
often represent the central target of inquiry
(\citeproc{ref-domingue2024}{Domingue et al., 2024};
\citeproc{ref-mccabe2022}{McCabe et al., 2022};
\citeproc{ref-rohrer2021}{Rohrer \& Arslan, 2021}). Whenever researchers
ask whether an effect is moderated, or whether it holds only under
certain conditions, they are asserting that the impact of one variable
depends on another.

In psychology, this scale dependence has a characteristic source in
measurement. Researchers routinely conceptualize constructs such as
cognitive ability or symptom severity as continuous latent dimensions
(\citeproc{ref-embretsonReise2000}{Embretson \& Reise, 2000};
\citeproc{ref-wagenmakers2012}{Wagenmakers et al., 2012}). Those
dimensions are rarely observed directly; what reaches the data are
bounded, discrete quantities, such as counts, ratings, and accuracy
scores that carry floors, ceilings, and chance levels. Each measurement
scale imposes its own mapping from the latent dimension to the observed
response. Where the mapping compresses, a constant effect on the latent
dimension appears as a shrinking effect on the observed response: a
group or condition closer to a bound has less room to move, and can look
less affected by a manipulation, than a group or condition farther away
from bounds (e.g., Figure~\ref{fig-simple-count-example}).

Generalized linear models (GLMs) make this mapping explicit and give it
a standard vocabulary (\citeproc{ref-mccullaghnelder1989}{McCullagh \&
Nelder, 1989}; \citeproc{ref-nelderwedderburn1972}{Nelder \& Wedderburn,
1972}), separating two choices that are easy to conflate, (1) the
outcome family, which describes the response, and (2) the link function,
which sets the scale on which effects combine (Box 1).

\begin{tcolorbox}[enhanced jigsaw, bottomtitle=1mm, left=2mm, leftrule=.75mm, opacityback=0, colbacktitle=quarto-callout-note-color!10!white, colframe=quarto-callout-note-color-frame, colback=white, breakable, toptitle=1mm, opacitybacktitle=0.6, titlerule=0mm, bottomrule=.15mm, title={Box 1: Generalized linear models: outcome family and link function}, arc=.35mm, rightrule=.15mm, toprule=.15mm, coltitle=black]

The \emph{outcome family} is the distribution assumed for the response.
It defines which values the outcome can take and how observations vary
around their expectation. The \emph{link function}, \(g(\cdot)\), maps
the expected outcome, \(\mu_i = E(Y_i)\), onto the linear predictor,
\(\eta_i\), the scale on which the model is additive. With two
predictors and their interaction term,
\[g(\mu_i) = \eta_i, \qquad \eta_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_i + \beta_2 Z_i + \beta_3 X_i Z_i.\]

The inverse link, \(g^{-1}(\cdot)\), maps this additive structure back
onto the expected response. Its familiar role is to keep expected values
within the admissible range of a constrained outcome: \(\exp(\eta)\)
keeps expected counts positive, and the inverse logit keeps
probabilities between 0 and 1. Less obviously, this also fixes what the
absence of an interaction, \(\beta_3 = 0\), means in the fitted model.
The product-term coefficient \(\beta_3\) is therefore an interaction on
the link scale. A response-scale interaction is a different quantity: a
difference between expected differences on the observed outcome, for
example, whether the group difference in expected errors changes with
age in Figure~\ref{fig-simple-count-example}. Unless the link is the
identity, the two quantities can disagree
(\citeproc{ref-ainorton2003}{Ai \& Norton, 2003};
\citeproc{ref-mccabe2022}{McCabe et al., 2022};
\citeproc{ref-mize2019}{Mize, 2019}). Each family has a canonical link,
which is the one that arises directly from the family's mathematical
form, but it is a convenient default and other links are legitimate. A
Gaussian linear model is the special case that combines a Gaussian
family with an identity link, so the additive scale and the response
scale coincide and the scale question is invisible. The model behind
Figure~\ref{fig-simple-count-example} is a Poisson family with a log
link. The family respects that errors are non-negative counts, and the
link places additivity on the log scale.

\end{tcolorbox}

Often the outcome family is chosen with care while the link function is
left at its software default, fixing the scale of additivity without
deliberation. Two models can share a family and still place additivity
on different scales. For example, a Gamma family suits response times;
within that family, a log link makes effects additive on the log of the
mean response time, a proportional change, and the canonical inverse
link makes them additive in reciprocal means, a change in speed. A
binomial family fits correct-versus-incorrect responses and leaves the
link open among logit, probit, chance-corrected, and other forms.

The scale dependence of interactions has long been recognized. Loftus
(\citeproc{ref-loftus1978}{1978}) showed that interactions involving
response probabilities are meaningful only relative to a mapping between
observed and theoretical scales; Cox (\citeproc{ref-cox1984}{1984})
formalized interaction as a model-relative concept tied to
transformation and interpretation. Busemeyer and Jones
(\citeproc{ref-busemeyer1983}{1983}) carried the same point into the
measurement model, where the conclusion about how two variables combine
depends on the model assumed to link responses to the quantities they
measure. Lubinski and Humphreys (\citeproc{ref-lubinski1990}{1990})
showed that the specification choices alone can produce the appearance
of an interaction where the generating process contains none.

More recently, Wagenmakers et al. (\citeproc{ref-wagenmakers2012}{2012})
separated removable from nonremovable interactions. A removable
interaction can be undone by a monotonic transformation of the dependent
variable, whereas a nonremovable one cannot. In this regard, J. C. Dunn
and Anderson (\citeproc{ref-dunnanderson2026}{2026}) developed a
removability test for linear models, while Rohrer and Arslan
(\citeproc{ref-rohrer2021}{2021}) showed that the same interaction can
be positive, null, or negative depending on the scale chosen for the
outcome, so the appropriate test follows from the scale on which the
hypothesis is posed. Rimpler and colleagues
(\citeproc{ref-rimpler2026}{2026}) added that a product term assumes one
predictor's effect changes at a constant rate across levels of the
other, and so misrepresents any dependence that does not take that form,
such as a threshold effect. Because the product-term coefficient of a
GLM refers to the link scale (Box 1), a question about the response
scale is answered with marginal effects (\citeproc{ref-ainorton2003}{Ai
\& Norton, 2003}; \citeproc{ref-mccabe2022}{McCabe et al., 2022};
\citeproc{ref-mize2019}{Mize, 2019}; \citeproc{ref-rohrer2026}{Rohrer \&
Arel-Bundock, 2026}).

Domingue and colleagues (\citeproc{ref-domingue2024}{2024}) demonstrated
that outcome-model misspecification can produce false interaction
findings for binary, count, and censored outcomes, and Liddell and
Kruschke (\citeproc{ref-liddellkruschke2018}{2018}) showed that treating
ordinal responses as metric can inflate error rates for interactions.
Czado and Santner, and later Yu and Huang, studied the inferential
consequences of link misspecification in binary GLMs and GLMMs
(\citeproc{ref-czadosantner1992}{Czado \& Santner, 1992};
\citeproc{ref-yuhuang2019}{Yu \& Huang, 2019}).

Despite the above cited literature spanning decades, to our knowledge no
study has attempted to quantify how often applied psychological research
makes explicit the outcome family and link that define the
no-interaction baseline. The present article approaches that gap from
two directions. We first document current practice with a preregistered
review of recent empirical articles in five prominent psychology
journals, quantifying how often interactions are tested, how often the
outcome family and link function are made explicit, and which response
formats predominate. We then develop a framework that separates outcome
family, link function, and target metric, and work it through three
cases, forced-choice accuracy with a chance floor, the choice between
logit and probit links, and sum scores. Simulations quantify how often
an inadequate link turns a purely additive structure into a
statistically significant interaction, and whether standard diagnostics
detect the misspecification.

\section{Current Practice in Psychological Interaction Testing: A
Preregistered
Review}\label{current-practice-in-psychological-interaction-testing-a-preregistered-review}

The review asks how often psychological articles test interactions, and
how often they make the outcome family and link function explicit enough
for a reader to know which interaction claim was tested. The coding is
descriptive, so it documents reporting practice rather than the
correctness of any substantive conclusion.

\subsection{Review Method}\label{review-method}

The protocol was preregistered before any record was screened
(\url{https://osf.io/bdu73/}), together with the coding data dictionary.
The essentials are given here; the full protocol, sampling frame, and
reliability are in Supplement A.

Following the preregistration, we sampled one journal from each of five
areas: \emph{Psychological Science} (general), \emph{Journal of
Experimental Psychology: General} (experimental), \emph{Journal of
Personality and Social Psychology} (social), \emph{Developmental
Science} (developmental), and \emph{Psychological Medicine} (clinical).
The five were selected on preregistered discretionary criteria: a
first-quartile 2023 Impact Factor, a broad rather than narrowly
specialized scope, not primarily publishing reviews, and strong
reputation in their field, as jointly judged by the coauthors. From each
journal's full set of 2024 articles in Scopus, records were screened in
a fixed random order until 40 eligible articles testing at least one
interaction on an observed outcome were reached, yielding 200 coded
articles. The preregistered stopping rule referred to
interaction-testing articles in general; the restriction to observed
outcomes was introduced during implementation. Because screening stopped
at a fixed number of coded articles, the number of records screened, and
therefore the denominator of the eligibility proportion, varied across
journals. Eligible articles were empirical reports of original research;
reviews, meta-analyses, editorials, theoretical and qualitative-only
articles, replication-only studies, and psychometric validation studies
were excluded. The use of latent-variable or structural-equation models
was not a reason for exclusion. Articles whose interaction tests all
concerned latent outcomes did not count toward the 40 per journal,
because our family and link coding was defined for observed outcomes.
Note that latent-variable models do not remove the scale problem per se,
but move part of it to the measurement model and the definition of the
latent scale. We treat this restriction as a deviation from the protocol
and retain the resulting sample rather than reconstructing the point at
which screening would otherwise have stopped. It concerned 8 of the 208
eligible interaction-testing articles; articles combining
latent-variable analyses with at least one observed-outcome interaction
were coded.

Each of the coded articles was independently coded by two researchers,
with disagreements resolved by discussion or by consulting the other
coauthors. Generative AI tools were used only as an optional aid to
locate passages within an article; all coding used in the review is
human.

For each article, the coders recorded five things: whether at least one
tested interaction used a non-identity link; whether the link function
was explicit; whether at least one interaction applied a
Gaussian-identity analysis to a constrained outcome; whether at least
one such interaction was statistically significant, or interpreted
analogously in a Bayesian analysis; and which response-variable types
were analyzed.

By a Gaussian-identity analysis on a constrained outcome we mean a
Gaussian linear regression with an identity link applied to an outcome
with formal constraints, such as bounds, discreteness, positivity, or a
known chance level. This is a conservative screening flag, applied only
when the constraint was identifiable from the report and does not imply
the substantive conclusion is wrong. The label is defined relative to
the constrained process that generates the outcome (if the observed
score were explicitly justified as the target metric, identity-link
additivity would instead be a defensible modeling choice, a distinction
we develop in the following sections).

Interrater agreement on the double-coded records was high (Cohen's
\(\kappa\) between 0.66 and 0.81). Supplement A, Section S2, reports the
preregistered variable definitions, the operational coding rules
including borderline cases, per-variable agreement and coder margins,
and the by-journal results.

\subsection{Review Findings}\label{review-findings}

First, interaction testing was common. Of the 367 articles screened, 345
met the eligibility criteria, of which 208 tested at least one
interaction (60.3\%). Under the stopping rule this proportion describes
the records screened rather than a fixed-denominator sample, so we
report it descriptively and treat the per-journal proportions as the
more informative summary: these ranged from 39.4\% in Psychological
Medicine to 85.1\% in Journal of Experimental Psychology: General. Of
the interaction-testing articles, 8 (3.8\%) tested interactions only on
latent outcomes, and the remaining 200 formed the coded sample described
below.

Second, model-scale reporting was uncommon. Among the 200 coded
articles, 58 used at least one non-identity link function (29\%), and
only 46 made the link function explicit (23\%). The link function was
therefore not explicit in 77\% of coded articles.

Even the 46 cases rest on a rule looser than the preregistered one,
which required a declaration of the link and treated naming a family or
a generalized linear model as insufficient. In coding we also accepted
link-specific model names, because in practice they identify the link:
``logistic regression'' names the logit and ``probit regression'' the
probit. We also accepted ``Poisson regression'', which names a family
whose canonical link is the log. Conversely, ANOVA, ordinary regression,
and linear mixed models were not counted as explicit, although they
imply the identity link, because there the identity scale is the default
rather than a stated choice. The stricter preregistered rule would give
a lower count.

Third, constrained outcomes were often analyzed on the identity scale.

\citeproc{ref-liddellkruschke2018}{Liddell \& Kruschke, 2018}).

\subsection{What the Present Review Shows, What It Does
Not}\label{what-the-present-review-shows-what-it-does-not}

In these journals, then, interaction tests are common and the scale on
which no interaction is assumed is rarely stated. The link function was
explicit in only 23\% of coded articles, and 72\% applied an
identity-link analysis to a constrained outcome. Bounded outcomes also
dominated, with binary, accuracy, or proportion outcomes, sum scores,
and ordinal ratings together appearing in 152 of the 200 coded articles
(76\%). For approximately continuous outcomes, additivity on the
observed scale can be a defensible target, whereas for bounded and
discrete outcomes it is a stronger assumption, and treating them as
metric distorts estimates, error rates, and interaction conclusions
(\citeproc{ref-burknervuorre2019}{Bürkner \& Vuorre, 2019};
\citeproc{ref-liddellkruschke2018}{Liddell \& Kruschke, 2018}).

A descriptive review cannot establish how often published interactions
are scale artifacts, because that judgment requires knowing the scale on
which the data-generating process is additive. J. C. Dunn and Anderson
(\citeproc{ref-dunnanderson2026}{2026}) provide a direct test of
removability, but it requires cell-level summaries and error estimates
that the reviewed articles often did not report, and many designs used
mixed-effects models, whose output does not reduce to cell-level means.
The remainder of the paper therefore turns to simulations, where the
generating process is known by construction, and focuses on the binomial
and aggregated responses that dominated the corpus, including sums of
binary or ordinal items.

\section{The Link Function Problem in Three Common
Cases}\label{the-link-function-problem-in-three-common-cases}

To show what link misspecification does to interaction tests, we
conducted three simulation studies. Each asks whether a data-generating
process that is purely additive on one scale yields a statistically
significant interaction when it is modeled on another scale.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \emph{Forced-Choice Accuracy}: This case holds the binomial outcome
  family fixed and evaluates whether the link function must explicitly
  encode a task-implied chance floor (e.g., a .50 guessing threshold).
\item
  \emph{Within-Family Link Choice (Logit vs.~Probit)}: This case holds
  both the binary family and the 0-to-1 bounds fixed, testing whether
  choosing between these two standard link functions alters the results.
\item
  \emph{Sum Scores}: This case holds the latent generating process fixed
  and evaluates whether the bounded total shows an interaction that the
  latent scale does not.
\end{enumerate}

All three take up the case in which the outcome family is already
broadly plausible and the link alone sets a disputable no-interaction
baseline, which is the common situation for bounded and chance-limited
psychological outcomes. Work on misspecification has largely varied the
outcome model as a whole (\citeproc{ref-domingue2024}{Domingue et al.,
2024}; \citeproc{ref-liddellkruschke2018}{Liddell \& Kruschke, 2018}),
whereas here the link carries most of the disagreement about what counts
as no interaction.

The simulations share one design. Data are generated with no product
term on the generating scale, and models assuming additivity on that
scale and on plausible alternative scales are fitted to the same data.
The rate at which the matched model rejects a null product term is the
\emph{nominal rejection rate}. A product term estimated under a
mismatched model is a genuine property of that model's scale
(\citeproc{ref-cox1984}{Cox, 1984}; \citeproc{ref-domingue2024}{Domingue
et al., 2024}; \citeproc{ref-loftus1978}{Loftus, 1978};
\citeproc{ref-mccabe2022}{McCabe et al., 2022}), correct where it is
computed and misleading only when the claim is carried to the generating
scale. Because that scale is known in a simulation, we call such a
result a \emph{pseudo-interaction}, and the rate at which it reaches
significance the \emph{pseudo-interaction detection rate}.

The scenarios were chosen to represent plausible psychological data.
Each reproduces a common interaction-testing situation, in which two
well-established and large main effects, for example a group difference
combined with a condition effect or a developmental trend, raise the
question of whether one effect moderates the other. Most scenarios set
these effects strong enough to push expected values close to a floor,
ceiling, or chance level while keeping them inside the observed range,
which is where link curvature acts most strongly, and scenarios away
from the bounds serve as a comparison. Technical details are reported in
Supplement A, and Supplement B reports a broader sensitivity atlas that
varies sample size, main-effect magnitude, the intercept, and selected
design parameters. A final simulation, in the next section, examines
whether standard diagnostics identify the misspecified link.

\subsection{Forced-Choice Accuracy and the Non-Zero Chance
Floor}\label{forced-choice-accuracy-and-the-non-zero-chance-floor}

If a participant answers \(Y_i\) of \(k\) trials correctly, a binomial
sampling model, \(Y_i \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(k, p_i)\), is the natural
starting point. The family requires the expected accuracy to lie between
0 and 1. When guessing is a plausible process for the task, the range
that matters is narrower, since a participant who knows nothing still
gets a share \(c\) of the trials right, \(c = .50\) in a two-alternative
or true/false task and \(c = 1/m\) with \(m\) alternatives, so expected
accuracy lies between \(c\) and 1, even though observed proportions can
fall below \(c\) through sampling variation. Accuracy data of this kind
are analyzed in several ways in practice, on the raw proportion scale
with a Gaussian identity model, with a standard binomial logit or
probit, and, less often, with a link that encodes the chance floor.

These specifications differ in which of the two ranges they impose. The
Gaussian identity model imposes neither, since it ignores the trial
process, assumes additivity in percentage-correct units, and can predict
outside the 0-to-1 range. The other two keep the binomial family and
differ only in the link. The standard logit or probit,
\(p_i = F(\eta_i)\) with \(F\) the logistic or standard-normal CDF,
holds expected accuracy between 0 and 1 and places the lower asymptote
at 0, whereas the chance-corrected link maps the linear predictor onto
the narrower interval from \(c\) to 1, \(p_i = c + (1 - c)F(\eta_i)\),
which for \(c = .50\) becomes \(p_i = .50 + .50\,F(\eta_i)\). An
interaction term in the standard model therefore tests additivity over
the full 0-to-1 range, whereas the same term in the chance-corrected
model tests additivity on a performance-above-chance scale, so the same
four cell probabilities can imply different interactions on the three
scales (Figure~\ref{fig-motivating-example}).

The chance-corrected link is most defensible when the chance level is
known from the task design, guessing is a plausible lower-bound process,
and below-chance performance is not substantively meaningful for the
target claim. In the simplest case, the chance level can be fixed by
design, for example \(c = .50\) in a two-alternative task. In other
cases, analysts may estimate the lower asymptote or report sensitivity
analyses over plausible values of \(c\). Lapses, response biases, item
heterogeneity, changes across trials, or systematic below-chance
performance may instead require richer psychometric solutions such as
item-response models (e.g., \citeproc{ref-embretsonReise2000}{Embretson
\& Reise, 2000}; \citeproc{ref-knoblauch2012}{Knoblauch \& Maloney,
2012}; \citeproc{ref-wichmann2001}{Wichmann \& Hill, 2001}). For
example, the 3-parameter logistic Item Response Theory model estimates a
guessing parameter for each item, where the chance-corrected link fixes
a single chance level for the whole design
(\citeproc{ref-embretsonReise2000}{Embretson \& Reise, 2000}). The
chance-corrected link is thus a design-level approximation, and
item-level measurement models remain the appropriate tool when the data
and the scientific question call for that additional structure.

\subsubsection{Simulation 1: Design and
Results}\label{simulation-1-design-and-results}

In each of 3000 replications we generated 20 trial-level Bernoulli
responses for each of \(N = 250\) participants. Age and group were
constant across a participant's trials. The data-generating model
included a subject random intercept corresponding to a latent-scale ICC
of .30. The probability of a correct response followed a
chance-corrected logit with a .50 floor, a positive age effect, a group
difference, and no age-by-group product term on the conditional
chance-corrected-logit scale. Three scenarios differ only in overall
performance, set by shifting the fixed intercept. The deterministic
curves in Panel A are evaluated at random intercept 0.

Each simulated dataset was analyzed with four mixed models sharing the
same subject random intercept: a Gaussian identity LMM applied to the
binary trial outcome, standard binomial-logit and binomial-probit GLMMs,
and the matched chance-corrected binomial-logit GLMM. The matched model
was fitted with \texttt{lme4::glmer} and
\texttt{psyphy::mafc.logit(2)}\revstart, setting \texttt{nAGQ\ =\ 10}
(default 1), which evaluates the likelihood more accurately at some
computational cost, because with the default up to nearly half of these
fits were flagged (Supplement A)\revend. Each model received one fit
attempt. We report rejection rates separately for fits passing basic
convergence checks and for fits flagged by numerical problems, because
the latter can yield unreliable Wald tests. Exact criteria are given in
Supplement A.

For the matched chance-corrected model, the primary rate is the nominal
rejection rate among numerically acceptable fits. For the three
alternative links it is the pseudo-interaction detection rate among
acceptable fits. Rates among flagged fits are descriptive apparent
rejection rates, not valid inferential error rates.

The matched chance-corrected model rejected the product term in 5\% to
6\% of acceptable fits. The alternative links produced
pseudo-interactions, most often near the chance floor. In the
lower-performance scenario the standard logit detected an interaction in
20\% of replications, the standard probit in 19\%, and the Gaussian
identity in 12\%. In the higher-performance scenario these rates fell to
11\%, 8\%, and 5\%.

The matched model was also the hardest to fit. \revstart Even with
\texttt{nAGQ\ =\ 10}, from\revend{} 7\% to 12\% of its fits were
flagged, against at most 4\% for the alternatives. Flagged fits are not
a minor nuisance here, because they rejected the product term in 75\% to
78\% of cases. An analyst who ignored the warnings would therefore
obtain a pseudo-interaction rate of 10\% to 14\% from the model that is
correct by construction\revstart, comparable to some of the wrong
links\revend. The chance-corrected link is the ideal solution in
principle, but in a GLMM \revstart it requires attention to fitting
options and convergence warnings\revend.

\subsection{Within-Family Link Choice}\label{within-family-link-choice}

In the forced-choice case the standard link was wrong for the task by
construction, because it placed the lower asymptote at 0 when the design
fixed it at chance. The link-function problem can also arise in less
obvious cases. Logit and probit are both standard for binary data,
respect the same 0-to-1 bounds, and usually produce very similar fitted
probabilities. They differ mainly in interpretation, since logit
coefficients are log-odds, whereas probit coefficients are expressed on
a standard-normal latent scale, which makes probit the usual choice when
modeling psychological quantities such as ability, signal strength, and
response tendency (\citeproc{ref-dunn2018}{P. K. Dunn \& Smyth, 2018}).
The choice between them is therefore often guided by convention,
interpretation, or software, with little expected impact on substantive
conclusions (\citeproc{ref-agresti2002}{Agresti, 2002};
\citeproc{ref-yuhuang2019}{Yu \& Huang, 2019}). Yet even two nearly
interchangeable links define slightly different scales of additivity.
Equal differences under one link are not exactly equal under the other,
especially toward the tails, and this alone can alter the estimated
product term.

\subsubsection{Simulation 2: Design and
Results}\label{simulation-2-design-and-results}

In each of 3000 replications we generated repeated binary responses for
\(N = 700\) participants in a two-group, two-condition design, with 15
trials per participant-condition cell and a subject random intercept
(latent intraclass correlation .30). The large sample was chosen so that
the small systematic discrepancy between logit and probit links would be
detectable, and it is larger than in a typical psychological study. The
condition-by-group interaction term was zero on the generating link
scale. We crossed the generating link with the fitted link, using logit
and probit in both roles, and fitted every model as a random-intercept
GLMM, recording how often each returned a significant condition-by-group
interaction term at \(\alpha = .05\).

When the fitted link matches the generating link, the nominal rejection

0-to-1 bounds, and nearly overlapping fitted curves in the middle of the
probability range, the setting many applied analysts treat as
interchangeable.

These rates reflect a fixed quantity. Under the wrong link the same cell
probabilities imply a small but deterministic nonzero product term on
the fitted scale. Re-expressing the cell probabilities of each
generating model on the other link scale gives a product term of
\(-0.112\) for logit-generated data fitted with a probit and \(0.216\)

and \(0.078\)), because changing the link also changes how the random
intercept enters the model, but they remain a fixed, nonzero property of
the fitted scale. Supplement B varies the intercept, which shifts where
the four cell probabilities sit between 0 and 1, and shows that
detectability changes non-monotonically with it, so the same link
mismatch is nearly undetectable at some probability levels and much
easier to detect at others.

\subsection{Sum Scores}\label{sum-scores}

Many psychological outcomes are computed as total sum scores across
questionnaire items, task items, symptoms, errors, or ratings. In the
two cases above the outcome was a directly observed response, and only
the link was in question. A sum score is different, because the score
itself is already the result of an analytic choice. What is observed are
the individual item responses, which are binary or ordinal; the total is
a summary of them, produced by a scoring rule that gives every item the
same weight and every point of the scale the same width. In
psychological research the construct of interest is often the
underlying, generally continuous dimension, so the total can be seen as
a remapped version of that continuum. The link question then arrives
together with a prior question about which metric the claim is about.

The bounds matter because the construct may have no floor or ceiling,
whereas the total score has both. The relation between the two can be
described by how much change on the latent dimension is needed to raise
the total by one point, and this amount is not constant. In the middle
of the score range a small latent change moves the total by a point,
because many items are close to changing category. Near either bound a
much larger latent change is needed, because most items are already at
their lowest or highest category. A constant latent effect therefore
translates into fewer raw-score points as the total approaches the floor
or the ceiling, and the same holds wherever the instrument is less
informative along the latent dimension, so the effect is not limited to
the endpoints. A Gaussian identity model fitted to the total assumes the
opposite, namely that a predictor shifts the score by the same number of
points at every level of the other predictor. When one group lies closer
to a bound, a predictor with the same latent effect in both groups moves
that group's score by fewer points, and the model reports the difference
as an interaction.

The problem we raise sits next to a recent, wider debate about whether
sum scores should be replaced by factor scores or latent-variable
models. Some argue that sum scores are transparent, reliable enough for
many purposes, comparable across studies, and tied to how instruments
are scored in applied settings (\citeproc{ref-widaman2023}{Widaman \&
Revelle, 2023}); the counterargument is that a total encodes strong and
usually untested assumptions about how items represent the construct
(\citeproc{ref-mcneish2023}{McNeish, 2023};
\citeproc{ref-mcneish2020}{McNeish \& Wolf, 2020}). Without entering
that debate, we add a narrower point that it leaves aside. A model of
the manifest total defines the absence of an interaction in raw-score
points, so an interaction found there is an interaction on the score,
and it becomes a claim about the construct only under the additional
assumption that the two are linearly related.

When item-level data are available, the measurement model itself can
instead be modeled explicitly (\citeproc{ref-burknervuorre2019}{Bürkner
\& Vuorre, 2019}). Often, however, researchers deliberately work with
the total score for the practical reasons listed above. One option is
then a family that respects the bounds and the discreteness of the
total, for example a beta-binomial likelihood for a bounded count
(\citeproc{ref-wilcox1981}{Wilcox, 1981}). Here we use a deliberately
unusual alternative that changes only the link, keeping the observed
total under a Gaussian likelihood, rescaling it to the proportion of the
maximum score, and applying a probit link, as in Simulation 3 below.
This is an \emph{ad hoc} device outside standard practice, and it
illustrates whether the interaction conclusion changes if additivity is
placed on a nonlinear scale that compresses the expected values toward
the score bounds. It remains a model of the total, it will not recover
the construct, and the Gaussian likelihood is still misspecified in its
variance, since a bounded score has a variance that depends on its mean,
so the probit link changes only the scale on which additivity is defined
and leaves the distributional form as it is. Read against the sum-score
debate, it is a precaution for researchers who have decided to analyze
totals anyway and who accept that the interaction still needs a scale on
which additivity is plausible.

\subsubsection{Simulation 3: Design and
Results}\label{simulation-3-design-and-results}

In each of 3000 replications, we generated data for \(N = 600\)
participants from a continuous latent variable with a continuous
predictor \(x\), a group effect, and no x-by-group interaction. The
latent values generated 9 ordinal items scored 0 to 3, which were summed
to a total ranging from 0 to 27. Item thresholds were shifted across
three scenarios so that expected totals fell in the lower, middle, or
upper part of the score range.

We analyzed each total in three ways: a Gaussian identity model of the
observed sum score, a Gaussian-probit bounded-score model, and the true
latent generating scale. The bounded-score model applies a probit link
to a continuity-corrected score proportion and rescales predictions to
the original sum-score metric. We used probit because its
standard-normal linear predictor provides a simple correspondence with
the latent scale. We recorded the proportion of significant x-by-group
interactions at \(\alpha = .05\). Results are shown in
Figure~\ref{fig-sum-score-simulation}, with per-scenario rates in
Supplement A, Section S4.

The latent benchmark remained close to the nominal .05 rejection rate in

its rate rose as expected totals approached a bound. Compression of the
latent scale makes the same latent group difference appear to change
across levels of the predictor. The bounded-score probit model rejected
in 8\%, 3\%, and 4\% of replications in the same three ranges. Its
predicted means respect the score bounds, although it is still a model
of the manifest total.

This pattern mainly depends on where observations fall within the score
range. Across the broader scenarios in Supplement B, the position of
expected scores within the range affected identity-model rejection much
more than sample size, item count, or latent dispersion. Raw-score
additivity can approximate latent-scale additivity when scores remain
away from the bounds. The bounded-score probit model can therefore serve
as a sensitivity analysis. For claims about interactions between latent
constructs, an explicit measurement model provides a more direct
approach, for example SEM, item-level ordinal models, or IRT-inspired
models. These approaches still depend on assumptions about thresholds,
links, loadings, and latent-response scales, but they make those
assumptions explicit.

\section{Can Standard Diagnostics Flag a Wrong
Link?}\label{can-standard-diagnostics-flag-a-wrong-link}

The simulations above assume that the analyst accepts the fitted model.
In practice, a researcher who finds an interaction may check the model
before reporting it. We examined three checks based on different
strategies. A Pregibon-style link test probes the assumed link, residual
diagnostics screen for misfit of any kind, and AIC compares candidate
models.

The Pregibon test refits the model adding the squared fitted linear
predictor, \(\hat{\eta}^2\), as a predictor
(\citeproc{ref-pregibon1980}{Pregibon, 1980}). If the link is adequate,
this term adds no information. A significant coefficient indicates
curvature that the link does not explain. For residual diagnostics, we
used the simulated quantile residuals of DHARMa
(\citeproc{ref-hartig2024}{Hartig, 2024}; see also
\citeproc{ref-dunnsmyth1996}{P. K. Dunn \& Smyth, 1996}). Under an
adequate model they are uniform between 0 and 1, whatever the family.
Misfit appears as non-uniformity, over- or underdispersion, or patterns
over fitted values and predictors. AIC compares only the models it is
given. Our candidate models share the response, the family, and the
formula, and differ only in the link. They therefore have the same
number of parameters, and AIC ranks them by log-likelihood alone.

None of the above checks is specific to the link. A significant Pregibon
term targets the link, but it also flags any omitted nonlinearity or
interaction. Moreover, the product term absorbs part of the curvature
produced by a wrong link. With a continuous predictor, some curvature
remains within groups and can in principle be detected. In a saturated
\(2 \times 2\) design no curvature remains in the fixed effects, because
any link can reproduce the four cell means.

The monotonic linear model of J. C. Dunn and Anderson
(\citeproc{ref-dunnanderson2026}{2026}) tests scale dependence more
directly, asking whether additivity can be recovered under any monotonic
transformation. All pseudo-interactions simulated here are removable in
this sense. However, the test requires cell-level data from a factorial
design and has no straightforward extension to mixed models, whereas
most of our scenarios include continuous predictors, trial-level random
effects, or both.

The five scenarios are based on examples introduced earlier; sample
sizes and coefficients are reported in Supplement A. Each scenario keeps
the outcome family and the interaction formula fixed and changes only
the link. The fitted model is therefore wrong about the scale on which
effects add, and about nothing else. \emph{Count errors} follows the
Poisson-log example of Figure~\ref{fig-simple-count-example} and fits it
with an identity link. \emph{Chance-floor accuracy} takes the
lower-performance scenario of Simulation 1, with trial-level responses
and a subject random intercept, and compares standard-logit and
chance-corrected-logit GLMMs with the same random effects. \emph{Binary
repeated trials} follows the probit-generated, logit-fitted case of
Simulation 2 with 300 participants instead of 700. \emph{Binary
continuous predictor} repeats that case with a continuous predictor
instead of the two-level condition, so that residual patterns can be
examined along a predictor. \emph{Gamma mean response time} generates
response times from a Gamma model with a log link and fits the inverse
link.

In each replication we applied the checks to the misspecified
interaction model, because this is the model an analyst would inspect
after finding a significant interaction. The residual checks covered
uniformity, dispersion, quantile patterns over fitted values, and
patterns over the focal predictor. The last was assessed with a quantile
check for a continuous predictor and with a comparison across design
cells in the \(2 \times 2\) case. \revstart All residual checks were run
with the package defaults (DHARMa 0.5.0).\revend{} AIC compared the
misspecified model with the target model. We also applied the residual
checks and the Pregibon test to the correctly specified model fitted to
the same data, to see how often each flags a model that is right.

\section{From Link Choice to Sensitivity
Analysis}\label{from-link-choice-to-sensitivity-analysis}

In practice, an interaction analysis should state the three choices it
depends on: the outcome family, the link that defines additivity, and
the metric the claim refers to. It should also test whether its
conclusion holds under plausible alternatives
(Table~\ref{tbl-link-aware-workflow}). Sometimes the observed metric is
the relevant target. For example, a two-point difference on a clinical
or educational score may be what matters in practice, and additivity in
raw-score units may then be reasonable. The point is that this is a
substantive choice, which should be made deliberately and reported. When
possible, the link should be justified by theory, task structure, or
measurement.

When the link can affect the interaction conclusion, we suggest a
sensitivity analysis across plausible links as the main robustness
check. The removability test of J. C. Dunn and Anderson
(\citeproc{ref-dunnanderson2026}{2026}) addresses the same problem
directly, testing any monotonic transformation of the outcome. However,
besides having no straightforward extension to mixed models, it does not
decide which transformations are admissible. The family, the bounds, the
chance level, and the meaning of the outcome strongly restrict the
plausible mappings. We suggest testing the interaction under each
defensible link, reporting the corresponding response-scale contrast,
and stating whether the conclusion is stable or depends on the link. If
it depends on the link, this should be disclosed
(\citeproc{ref-mccabe2022}{McCabe et al., 2022};
\citeproc{ref-mize2019}{Mize, 2019};
\citeproc{ref-mustillo2018}{Mustillo et al., 2018};
\citeproc{ref-rohrer2026}{Rohrer \& Arel-Bundock, 2026}).

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

The same data can support different interaction conclusions under
different plausible specifications, which is the familiar scale
dependence of interactions (\citeproc{ref-cox1984}{Cox, 1984};
\citeproc{ref-dunnanderson2026}{J. C. Dunn \& Anderson, 2026};
\citeproc{ref-loftus1978}{Loftus, 1978};
\citeproc{ref-wagenmakers2012}{Wagenmakers et al., 2012}). In a GLM that
dependence is carried by two choices, the outcome family and the link
function. Our review found interaction tests to be common in all five
journals sampled, the link function usually left implicit, and
identity-link analyses applied to many outcomes for which observed-scale
additivity is not self-evident.

The simulations then showed what that can cost. With two main effects
present, a link that misplaces additivity turned a purely additive
generating process into a significant product term even when the outcome
family was correct. In forced-choice accuracy the binomial family is
correct and, in the simulated process, the error lies entirely in a
canonical link that places the lower asymptote at 0 rather than at the
chance level. The matched chance-corrected model, however, \revstart was
the hardest to fit, and its flagged fits produced spurious interactions
in most cases, so the ideal model requires care to be a usable
one\revend. The logit-probit comparison showed that the problem survives
when the response bounds are correct as well, between two links that
look alike over most of the range. In the sum-score case the choice
extends beyond the link to the outcome itself. A total is a scoring rule
applied to item responses, so an interaction estimated on it refers to
raw-score points, and adopting a family that respects the bounds and the
discreteness of the total still leaves open whether additivity in points
stands for additivity in the construct.

This difficulty is of a different kind from the one documented in work
on the precision of interaction estimates
(\citeproc{ref-baranger2023}{Baranger et al., 2023};
\citeproc{ref-castillo2026}{Castillo et al., 2026}). There the obstacle
is noise, and more data helps. Here the product term induced by the
wrong link is a fixed property of that scale, so a larger sample
estimates it more precisely and reports it as significant more often.

The diagnostic simulation asks whether ordinary post-fit checks flag
link misspecification. Residual checks, Pregibon-style tests, and AIC
answer different questions, and AIC can only rank the links it is given.
Outside the count scenario, residual checks rarely detected the wrong
link, and the Pregibon test did so in at most a third of replications.
In the chance-floor case they even flagged the correct model more often
than the wrong one, so a clean diagnostic report did not mean that the
scale was right. More generally, these diagnostics answer a question
about fit within a supplied candidate set, which is different from
whether an interaction claim holds on the scale the researcher means
(\citeproc{ref-dunnsmyth1996}{P. K. Dunn \& Smyth, 1996};
\citeproc{ref-hartig2024}{Hartig, 2024};
\citeproc{ref-pregibon1980}{Pregibon, 1980}).

Three limitations bound the scope of these conclusions. The review was
descriptive and covered five prominent journals across major areas of
psychology in a single year, so its estimates describe reporting
practice in such outlets and leave the field as a whole unmeasured, and
a broader mapping across journals, years, and subfields would be needed
to support reporting guidance. The review also coded observable
reporting features and did not assess removability in each article. The
simulations, finally, are demonstrations of a mechanism. They target a
single regime, in which two established main effects push expected
values into the region where link curvature is strongest, and they say
nothing about how often that regime arises or about the further
complications of real data.

To conclude, interaction claims rest on a scale that is rarely stated.
Naming it separates three questions that a software default otherwise
settles at once, which family suits the response, which link defines
additivity, and which metric the claim is about. The robustness question
then takes a definite form, whether the conclusion survives the links
that remain defensible. A result that holds across them is robust to
that choice. One that does not is still a result, and it should be
reported as conditional on the scale on which it was estimated.
